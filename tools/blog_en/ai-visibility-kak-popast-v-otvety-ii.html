Not long ago, a customer typed "dental clinic in Batumi" into a search engine and saw ten links. Today a noticeable share of people ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity the same question and get a single answer with three or four names. If your company isn't there, you don't exist for that person, whatever position you hold in classic search. This new field is called AI Visibility — how visible a business is in the answers of AI assistants. Let's look at where assistants get information about companies, what affects it and what you can do on your own.

How an AI assistant decides who to name

It helps to understand what happens when a user asks an assistant about a business. There are two fundamentally different modes.

An answer from the model's memory. The model was trained on a huge body of text and remembered what appeared often and consistently. If there are three mentions of your company on the internet, it most likely isn't in the model's memory. You can't influence this layer quickly: models are updated with a lag of months.

An answer with real-time search. For questions about local businesses, prices and services, most assistants go to search, read several pages and assemble an answer from them. This layer responds to changes within days and weeks, and this is where most of the work can be done.

In both cases the assistant prefers sources that answer the question directly, in a structured way, and agree with one another. This is the key difference from classic SEO: the assistant doesn't need ten of your pages, it needs one clear and consistent answer.

Step one: find out what AI knows about you now

Before changing anything, it's worth measuring the starting point. The simplest way is to ask several assistants the questions your customer would ask: "which companies make custom kitchens in Tbilisi", "what does such-and-such company do", "how much does their service cost", "what are their reviews like". Write down the answers: whether you were named, what was said, whether you were confused with someone else, whether outdated information came up.

Doing this by hand across five assistants and twenty questions is tedious, which is why services have appeared that do it automatically. But the principle matters more than the tool: measure first, then change.

Typical findings at this stage: the assistant knows the company but gives an old address; confuses it with a firm of the same name in another city; doesn't know about half the services because they are described only inside a PDF price list.

What affects visibility: five factors

1. Consistency

The name, address, phone number and description of what you do must match everywhere: on the website, on maps, in directories, on social media. If you are a "studio" on the website, a "salon" on maps and "Ivanov, sole proprietor" in a directory, the assistant may not put these together into one entity.

2. Mentions on third-party sites

An assistant places more trust in what others write about you: reviews on maps, mentions in local media, industry directories, community discussions. One honest review of your service on a specialist site often carries more weight than ten pages on your own website.

3. Direct answers to questions

A "Services" page full of generalities is useless to an assistant. A page titled "How much does an iPhone 15 screen replacement cost in Batumi and how long does it take", with a specific answer in the first paragraph, is what it will cite.

4. Technical readability

The site should serve its text in plain HTML rather than render it with scripts after loading; allow assistant bots access; and contain structured markup. This is covered in detail in the article on llms.txt, schema.org and robots.txt.

5. Freshness

Assistants with search take the update date into account. A 2023 price list and two-year-old "news" lower trust in the page as a whole.

What content assistants cite

If you look at which pages assistants link to in answers about businesses, a pattern emerges. It is almost always:

  • Pages with prices and terms — specific figures or ranges, timelines, what is included.
  • Comparisons and "how to choose" guides — an honest review of the options, including those where your product isn't the best choice.
  • Answers to frequent questions — not a three-item marketing FAQ, but real customer questions with detailed answers.
  • Case descriptions — what the task was, what was done, what the result was, with the limitations.
  • An about page with facts: year founded, city, team, specialization, what you don't do.

The general principle: the assistant looks for text it can relay to the customer as an answer. With texts like "we are a team of professionals with an individual approach", there is nothing to relay.

Example: a clinic with three specialties

A small clinic in Georgia: dentistry, cosmetology, diagnostics. A site on a website builder, prices in a PDF, reviews on only one map service. The check showed that assistants know the clinic as a dental practice and don't know about cosmetology at all; one of them gives a phone number that hasn't worked for two years.

What was done in a month: the name and contacts were made uniform across all platforms; prices were moved out of the PDF onto separate pages for each service, with the answer to "how much does it cost and how long does it take" in the first paragraph; organization and service markup was added; five detailed answers were written to the questions most often put to the front desk by phone; customers were asked to leave reviews on two more platforms.

A month and a half to two months later, a repeat check showed that assistants name the clinic for cosmetology queries and give the current phone number. These timelines are approximate; they depend on how often assistants re-index your region.

What not to do

  • Writing made-up reviews or "articles" about yourself on junk sites. Assistants cross-check sources, and contradictions lower trust in all of your data.
  • Generating hundreds of pages for every query. This worked in old SEO for a while; in AI answers, repetitive thin content doesn't get cited.
  • Hiding information behind an inquiry form. "Ask a manager for the price" tells the assistant that you have no price, and it will name a competitor who does.
  • Blocking assistant bots from the site out of caution. For some businesses this is a deliberate decision, but then you shouldn't expect visibility.

How to tie this in with the rest of your marketing

AI Visibility is not a separate discipline but a consequence of good content and orderly company data. The same texts with direct answers work in regular search, in newsletters and as a knowledge base for an AI assistant on your website. If content is produced systematically, visibility in AI answers becomes a side effect rather than a separate project. How to set up such a system without an agency is covered in the article on AI for small business marketing.

Where to start

  1. Make a list of 15–20 questions your customers ask and check the answers of several assistants. Record the result.
  2. Fix the discrepancies in name, address, phone number and description across all platforms.
  3. Put prices, timelines and terms into text on the site, one page per service.
  4. Write five detailed answers to your customers' most frequent questions.
  5. Check the technical side: bot access, markup, readable HTML.
  6. In a month or two, repeat the check with the same list of questions and compare.

Keep expectations realistic: visibility in AI answers grows over weeks and months, not days, and no one can guarantee a place in a particular answer. But companies that have put their data in order and write in specifics show up in answers noticeably more often than those that haven't.
