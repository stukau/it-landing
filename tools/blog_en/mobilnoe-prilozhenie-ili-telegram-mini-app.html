In 2026 the request "we need a mobile app" is almost always worth rephrasing: "we need the customer to be able to do something from a smartphone in a couple of taps." And from there it's a choice: a native app in the App Store and Google Play, or a Telegram Mini App that opens inside the messenger with no installation. The difference in cost, timelines and capabilities is substantial, and a wrong choice is expensive either way: either overpaying for the stores and two platforms where a Mini App would have been enough, or a Mini App where you need a camera, offline mode and system notifications. Let's go through the criteria that let you make the decision in a single meeting.

In short: what a Mini App is and what it isn't

A Telegram Mini App is a web application that opens inside Telegram from a button in a bot or from a link. To the user it looks like an ordinary messenger screen: no installation, no registration, no entering a phone number — Telegram already knows who they are. For the developer it's web technology with access to a number of Telegram features: user data, payments, interface buttons, the color theme, sending messages through a bot.

What a Mini App is not: it's not a full-fledged app with access to the phone's system. It has no home screen widgets, no background operation, no deep integration with the camera and files, it doesn't work without the internet and it depends on whether the customer has Telegram installed.

A comparison on the criteria that really affect the decision

The customer's path to the first action

Native app: see the link, go to the store, download, install, open, register, confirm the phone number. At every step some people drop off, and for a one-off or infrequent service few make it to the end. Mini App: tap a button in the chat and the person is already inside, authenticated. For a business whose customers don't use the service every day, this is the decisive argument.

Device capabilities

A native app has access to everything: the camera with image processing, the microphone, background geolocation, actionable notifications, widgets, a local database, biometrics, offline operation. A Mini App has access to a limited set: a basic camera through browser APIs, geolocation on request, notifications only through bot messages.

Payments

In a Mini App, payments go through Telegram: connected card providers, or Telegram Stars for digital goods. For many markets in the CIS and Georgia this works; for Europe the choice of providers is narrower. In native apps the stores take a commission on digital goods and subscriptions, but physical goods and services can be paid for through any payment processor.

Cost and timelines

A Mini App is one codebase, web technology and no store review. A first working version: roughly two to four weeks. A native app for iOS and Android means two platforms or a cross-platform framework, publication in the stores with review, and developer accounts. A first version: from a month to a month and a half, and that's with a narrow feature set. The difference in budget is usually severalfold.

Updates

A Mini App updates instantly for everyone: update the server and everyone sees the new version. A native app goes through store review, then users update gradually, and several versions live side by side.

Platform dependence

A Mini App exists as long as Telegram exists and as long as it's available in your market. A native app depends on the stores' rules, which also change. Neither option offers full independence.

When a Mini App is the obvious choice

Experience shows that a Mini App wins almost every time when three conditions are met: your customers are already on Telegram, they don't need deep device features, and the usage scenario is short.

Typical cases:

  • Appointments and bookings: a salon, a clinic, an auto repair shop, a gym. Choose a service, a time, confirm. Reminders arrive as messages from the bot.
  • Catalog and ordering: a cafe, a delivery service, a small shop. Menu, cart, payment, order status.
  • Loyalty program: points, history, promotions. People won't install a separate app for a coffee shop's bonuses, but they will open a Mini App in a chat.
  • Customer account: balance, documents, requests, statuses for the customers of a B2B company.
  • Aggregators and curated selections. Our example is Batumi Rent: an app inside Telegram that collects rental listings from channels, turns them into structured cards using a language model, removes duplicates and sends matching options. The audience is people who are looking for an apartment and already spend time in those same Telegram channels. Making them install a separate app would have been a mistake.

When you need a native app

There are tasks a Mini App can't handle, or will handle noticeably worse.

  • Heavy use of the camera and photos. Recognizing receipts and documents, scanning products. In My Stash, our family budget app, a receipt is photographed, recognized and turned into a list of purchases with amounts — this requires high-quality capture, on-device processing and local data storage.
  • On-device data and privacy. When the user doesn't want financial or medical information going to a server. A native app can store everything locally and work without the internet.
  • System integrations. On-screen widgets, reminders in the system calendar, voice input with recognition, background operation. SubMap, our subscription tracker, uses voice input, recognizes screenshots of subscriptions and reminds you about charges with system notifications — that experience can't be reproduced in a Mini App.
  • Daily use. If the product is opened several times every day, a home screen icon and a quick launch matter more than an easy first entry.
  • Markets where Telegram isn't the main messenger. In parts of Europe the audience is on WhatsApp, and a Mini App won't reach it. There's a separate article on choosing a platform by market: Telegram or WhatsApp.
  • A product that relies on app stores as a channel. If you're counting on search in the App Store and Google Play as a source of users, a Mini App won't give you that.

The hybrid path: start with a Mini App, grow into an app

These options aren't mutually exclusive. A sensible strategy for many companies: launch a Mini App as the first version of the product, test demand on real customers in two to four weeks, and only if the scenario is in demand and runs up against Telegram's limits, build a native app.

The back end — the database, the logic, the CRM and payment integrations — stays shared. The native app becomes a second interface to the same back end, not a project from scratch. This cuts the cost of the transition considerably, provided the architecture was designed with it in mind from the very beginning.

The reverse situation also occurs: a company already has an app, but customers from Telegram don't want to install it. Then a Mini App is built as a lightweight version with the core features on top of the same server.

Regional specifics

For an audience in the CIS and Georgia, Telegram is the main channel for communicating with customers, and a Mini App fits into it naturally. The customer already gets messages from the company in Telegram, and an "Open app" button in the same chat needs no explanation.

For Europe the picture is mixed: in some countries Telegram is widely used, in others it trails WhatsApp noticeably. If your customers are in different countries, it's worth checking this against your own customer base: which messenger people most often use to contact you. It's cheaper than guessing.

How to make the decision in a single meeting

Six questions whose answers usually settle the matter:

  1. Where do your customers talk to you today? If mostly on Telegram, a point for the Mini App.
  2. How often will one customer use the product? Less than once a week — a point for the Mini App. Daily — a point for the app.
  3. Do you need a camera with processing, offline operation, widgets, background processes? Yes — an app.
  4. Is the data so sensitive that it must be stored only on the device? Yes — an app.
  5. What are the budget and deadline for the first version? If you need to test the idea in a month and funds are limited — a Mini App.
  6. Does the app store matter as an acquisition channel? Yes — an app.

If the answers are split, the right answer is almost always to start with a Mini App and design the server with room to grow. What exactly to include in the first version and what to deliberately postpone is covered in our article on an MVP in two weeks.

The exceptions are products tied to the camera, voice and local data, like SubMap and My Stash, where the native platform provides what a messenger doesn't.
