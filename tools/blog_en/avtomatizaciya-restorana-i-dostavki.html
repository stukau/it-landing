Friday evening at a small restaurant with delivery looks the same almost everywhere. The phone rings non-stop, the host takes an order, shouts it to the kitchen, answers Instagram at the same time, and the courier waits at the counter because nobody knows whether the order that is ready is for table four or the one for delivery. Two hours later it turns out that one order was lost, another went out with the wrong sauce, and a guest who waited 50 minutes instead of the promised 30 has written a review.

Most of these problems are not about the kitchen or the people. They are problems of passing information: from the guest to the host, from the host to the kitchen, from the kitchen to the courier and back to the guest. That is exactly what automation solves. Let's go step by step through what can be built for a restaurant or delivery service with one to three locations, and what is better left alone.

Order taking: move the phone out of the centre of the process

As long as the phone is the main ordering channel, there is almost nothing to automate: the order exists only in the host's head until it is written down on a slip of paper. The first step is to move order taking to a place where the order is born in structured form straight away.

For a small restaurant, three options work, and they can be combined:

  • A Telegram bot or Mini App with a menu. The guest picks dishes, enters the address and time, and pays or chooses to pay on delivery. The order enters the system with no human involved. For Georgia and the CIS this is the most natural channel. How a Mini App differs from a regular bot is covered in the article Telegram Mini Apps: an app for your customers without the App Store.
  • A form on the website with the same menu. It is needed if a significant share of guests come from search or maps.
  • An AI agent in messengers for those who write in plain text anyway: "two Adjarian khachapuri and a lemonade, 20 Chavchavadze, by seven". The model parses the message into a structured order, asks for what is missing (entrance, payment method) and confirms the items and the price. Phone orders can be taken the same way through a voice assistant, but that is the next stage.

An important detail: the agent works from the current menu and stop list in the system. If a dish has run out, the agent suggests a replacement instead of accepting an order the kitchen cannot fulfil. The price is always taken from the database, never "recalled" by the model.

Kitchen: one screen instead of shouting and paper slips

Once orders arrive in structured form, they can be shown to the kitchen on a screen: what to cook, in what order, by what time. This is called a KDS (kitchen display system), and for a small restaurant it can be built on a tablet at a moderate cost or taken as a ready-made module in the POS system.

What this gives you in practice:

  • The cook sees orders in priority order: a delivery due at 7:00 PM ranks above a table that has just sat down.
  • The "ready" status is set with one tap and goes straight to the host, the courier and the guest.
  • The time from acceptance to handover is recorded for every order. After a month you can see which dishes slow the kitchen down at peak.

AI is barely needed here. The exception is load forecasting: based on order history, a model or simple statistics suggests how much prep to do for Friday evening and warns that by 8:00 PM the kitchen will not manage the promised 30 minutes, so the delivery time in the bot should be increased temporarily.

Delivery: the courier, the status and an honest time

A guest who knows the order is on its way does not call. The scheme is the same as in any logistics: the courier taps "picked up" and "delivered" in their own bot, the guest gets a notification with an estimated time, and the host sees who is where.

Restaurants have one specific: the promised time. It is better to promise 45 minutes and deliver in 40 than to promise 30 and deliver in 50. Automation lets you change the promised time dynamically: when the kitchen has more than a set number of orders in progress, the bot automatically shows a longer time. It is a single rule in n8n, and it removes a significant share of the negativity.

If delivery is handed over to aggregators, they already have part of this chain. But aggregator orders should also be brought into your own system — at least for record-keeping and analytics, otherwise the sales picture will be incomplete.

Feedback: catch dissatisfaction before a public review

Most bad reviews could have been prevented if the restaurant had learned about the problem first. Automation does it like this:

  1. 30–40 minutes after the "delivered" status, the guest gets a short message: "How did everything go?" with a rating from 1 to 5.
  2. A rating of 4–5 — a thank-you and, if desired, an invitation to leave a review on maps.
  3. A rating of 1–3 — an open question: "what went wrong?" The AI agent analyses the answer: cold, slow, wrong items, rude courier. It creates a task for the manager with a category and the text, and replies to the guest according to the script: an apology and, if the rules provide for it, a bonus on the next order.

After a month the manager has not a vague feeling that "people seem to complain about slowness" but a table: 60% of negative feedback is delivery time on Fridays, 25% is dishes that went cold on distant addresses. That is already a basis for decisions: a second courier at peak or a smaller delivery zone.

What the agent must not do: argue with the guest, promise compensation the manager has not approved, or respond to complaints about health and food quality without a human. Such cases are escalated immediately.

Winning guests back: messages that do not annoy

Once orders and contacts are gathered in one database, you get the chance to bring guests back. Not with a mass "10% off for everyone" mailing, but in a targeted way:

  • A guest ordered once a week and went quiet for three weeks — one message with a reminder and a small bonus.
  • A new dish in the category the guest orders most often — a short announcement just for them.
  • A birthday, if the guest provided the date — a greeting with a gift added to the order.

This is set up by linking a CRM or spreadsheet with the bot; more on the mechanics in the article on a Telegram bot for a loyalty programme. The general rule: no more than one or two messages a month per person, otherwise unsubscribes will eat up the whole effect.

What gets built in two weeks and what comes later

The temptation to build everything at once is strong, but in practice we at AiKrok recommend a sequence in which each stage works on its own:

Phase one (about two weeks): a bot with a menu and order taking, a kitchen screen or integration with the POS system, a courier bot, and status notifications for guests. This already eliminates lost orders and most of the calls.

Phase two: feedback collection with analysis of negative responses, basic reports on orders and handover times, and a stop list that updates the menu in the bot by itself.

Phase three: an AI agent for free-text orders, voice call handling, load forecasting and personalised messages.

If the restaurant already has a POS system with a delivery module, much of phase one is already there, and the job comes down to integration with messengers rather than development from scratch.

Limitations worth knowing about

Automation will not fix a kitchen that objectively cannot keep up: it will only show this in numbers. Nor will it replace personal contact in a restaurant with regulars, where the host knows everyone by name — there the bot is needed only for delivery. Voice order taking in several languages at once (Georgian, Russian and English in one city) is still unreliable, and at peak the phone is better left to a person, with everything else moved to automation.

Finally, any setup depends on the team's discipline. If the cook does not tap "ready" and the courier does not mark "delivered", the notifications lie, and guests stop trusting them. So for the first two weeks after launch someone has to make sure statuses are being set and show the team that this saves their own time.

Summary

Restaurant and delivery automation is, above all, order in how information is passed along: the order is born in the system, the kitchen sees the queue, the courier marks the status, the guest gets a notification and a chance to complain to you rather than on maps. AI is added on top of that order: it parses text orders, reads complaints and suggests the expected load. It is worth starting with order taking and statuses rather than a "smart assistant", and a month later comparing the number of lost orders and complaints about time with what you had before.
