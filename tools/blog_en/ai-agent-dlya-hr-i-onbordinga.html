In a company of 30–80 people, the HR function usually rests on one person, and sometimes on an office manager and an accountant doing it on the side. Yet the stream of identical questions doesn't depend on the size of the department: how to book leave, where to get a certificate, when payday is, how training costs are reimbursed, who to ask for email access. A new hire asks dozens of them in the first week; long-time employees regularly forget the answers. Each answer takes three minutes, but they add up to hours that HR could have spent on hiring and retaining people.

An AI agent for HR covers three layers of this routine: onboarding new hires, answering policy questions and taking standard requests. Below is how it looks in practice, what you need to prepare and where the line is beyond which a real person is needed.

Three tasks the agent handles well

Onboarding new employees

A new hire's first week is a string of questions, half of which they are embarrassed to ask colleagues for the third time. An agent in Telegram or the corporate messenger walks them through a checklist: which documents to sign, where to get access, who to meet, which policies to read by Friday. At the same time it answers any question — from "how do I connect to the printer" to "what's our dress code". The manager sees progress on the checklist and gets a notification if the new hire is stuck.

Questions about policies and procedures

The agent works on top of the company's knowledge base: policies on leave, business trips, reimbursements, approval procedures and safety rules. It answers briefly, with a reference to the clause in the document. This is the same RAG mechanism used for customer support; it is described in detail in the article on RAG in practice. The difference is the audience: here mistakes are seen by your own people, not by customers, which is why an HR assistant often becomes a company's first and safest AI project.

Routine requests

Leave, a day off, a certificate of employment, equipment replacement, system access, a business trip. The agent collects the required fields in conversation, checks the basic conditions — for example, the leave balance or submission deadlines — creates the request and sends it to the manager for approval. The employee checks the status with the same agent instead of messaging HR directly. A simple combination of n8n and the Telegram Bot API is enough to handle approvals; how such internal bots are built is covered in the article on a Telegram bot for internal processes.

What a day with the agent looks like

Picture a distribution company with 50 employees, two warehouses and an office.

  • 8:50 — a warehouse worker writes to the agent in Telegram: "I need a certificate for the bank." The agent clarifies the type of certificate and creates the request; the accountant gets a notification with the data ready.
  • 9:30 — a new sales manager asks where to find the price list and how to log a first deal in the CRM. The agent sends a link to the guide and reminds them that they have a meeting with their mentor at 12:00.
  • 11:15 — a driver asks whether he can take two days of leave next week. The agent checks the balance and creates the request; the manager approves it with one button.
  • 14:00 — an employee asks about reimbursement for training. The policy has a clause on it; the agent quotes it and says who to write to for course approval.
  • 16:40 — the accountant asks the agent how many leave requests have been submitted for August. The agent returns the list from the spreadsheet.

None of these requests reached the HR manager, but all of them are recorded and have a history.

What to prepare before launch

The agent won't make up for policies that don't exist. Before starting, you need to gather and tidy up:

  1. Up-to-date documents — policies, instructions, guides. Delete old versions or mark them clearly.
  2. An onboarding checklist by role: what each new hire does on the first day, in the first week and month. If you don't have one, you'll have to write it — and that is useful in itself.
  3. A list of standard requests with fields and an approval route: who approves leave, who grants access, who signs certificates.
  4. Access rules: which documents everyone can see and which are for managers only. Payroll records and personal data don't go into the knowledge base.
  5. A list of questions from the past month — from chats, email, HR's memory. This is the basis of the test set.

In practice, preparation takes from two days to a week, depending on the state of the documents. For many companies this is the first time the policies have been gathered in one place at all.

Sensitive data and limits of authority

HR is a personal data zone, and the agent needs clear boundaries.

  • The agent answers from policies and collects requests, but has no access to salaries, performance reviews, disciplinary records or medical data.
  • Employees' personal data stays in the existing system — 1C, Bitrix24, an HR platform — and the agent queries it selectively and only for its own tasks, for example to check a leave balance.
  • Where the law or company policy requires it, the language model is deployed locally — Llama, Qwen or Mistral on your own server — rather than in the cloud. The options are covered in detail in the article on data security when adopting AI.
  • Conversation logs are available to a limited group of people and stored for a limited time.

A separate point: the agent must not make value judgments about people and must not make personnel decisions. "Can we fire an employee for being late" is a question for a lawyer and a manager, and that is exactly what the agent has to say.

Where an HR agent won't help

Conflicts, dismissals, difficult personal situations, salary negotiations — everything where empathy and accountability matter stays with people. The agent can only take the request properly and pass it to HR marked "urgent, in person".

A company of ten people who all sit in one room will gain almost nothing: questions get resolved faster by talking. The threshold beyond which the agent starts to pay off is roughly several dozen employees, a distributed team or noticeable turnover, when onboarding repeats every month.

If the company's policies contradict its practice — "the policy says leave is approved two weeks in advance, but in reality it's a day" — the agent will faithfully quote the policy and irritate people. First fix the practice or the document, then automate.

How to measure the result

Useful metrics for an HR agent:

  • the share of questions the agent answered without HR involvement;
  • the average time from submitting a request to approval;
  • the share of new hires who completed the onboarding checklist on time;
  • the number of questions the agent couldn't find an answer to — this is your list of missing policies;
  • a usefulness rating from employees through a simple monthly survey.

That list of unanswered questions is perhaps the most valuable side effect: within a month the agent shows which documents the company is missing more precisely than any audit.

Where to start

The sensible order is not "all of HR at once" but one layer at a time. Companies usually start with policy questions: this requires only documents and gives a quick, visible effect. Two or three weeks later they add leave and certificate requests — the most frequent ones. Onboarding is switched on when a new employee is about to join and the process can be tried out on them.
