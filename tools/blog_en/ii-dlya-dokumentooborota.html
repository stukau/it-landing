Documents are the most underestimated cost item in a small business. They don't show up in the profit and loss statement, yet every day the accountant retypes supplier invoices by hand, a manager pieces a contract together from three old ones, and the company learns that its lease has expired from the landlord's letter. These are not people's mistakes — they are the result of documents arriving in different formats, through different channels, with nobody keeping every deadline in their head.

AI in document workflows handles three specific jobs: pulling data out of incoming documents, assembling a draft of a new document from a template, and sending a reminder about a deadline before it becomes a problem. Let's go through each one — with notes on where automation works and where it doesn't yet.

Recognizing invoices and delivery notes

How it worked before language models

Classic document recognition needed a template for every supplier: the number goes here, the amount goes there. A new supplier meant a new template. For a company with a hundred counterparties, that didn't work.

How it works now

Multimodal models (GPT-5, Claude, Gemini) read a scan or photo of the whole document and return structured data: counterparty, number, date, line items, amounts, VAT, bank details. The document format doesn't matter — an invoice from a new supplier is processed the same way as one from an old supplier. The data is then checked against your records: does this counterparty exist, does the amount match the order, has this number been seen before.

The processing flow for an incoming invoice usually looks like this:

  1. The document arrives by email, in Telegram, or is dropped into a folder.
  2. The model extracts the fields and returns them in a defined structure.
  3. Rules check that the counterparty is known, the amount is within the order, and the bank details match the ones used before.
  4. If everything matches, a draft document is created in 1C or another accounting system, and the accountant reviews and posts it. If not, the document goes into a queue for manual review with a note on exactly what didn't match.

The last step is essential. Checking bank details catches a common fraud scheme: a fake invoice from a familiar supplier with a changed bank account number. A person doesn't compare the account digits with past payments; the system does it every time.

Limitations

Poor phone photos — blurry, with glare — are recognized less accurately. Handwritten notes and corrections on documents almost always need a human. Multi-page specifications with dozens of line items are recognized, but an error in one line out of fifty is likely, so checking against the order is mandatory here. A sensible position: AI does the initial data entry and checks, the accountant gives the final confirmation. That removes the mechanical part, but not the responsibility. Drafts of contracts and standard documents

The second group of tasks is about creating documents rather than reading them. A supply contract, an acceptance certificate, an addendum, a commercial proposal, a power of attorney — in most companies these are ten to fifteen standard documents that differ in company details, amounts and a couple of variable terms.

What AI does

  • Takes the deal data from the CRM or from a manager's short description: "supply contract with such-and-such LLC for 12 months, 14-day deferred payment, delivery at our expense."
  • Inserts it into the company's template approved by a lawyer.
  • Selects the right variable clauses from a library: deferred payment, delivery terms, liability for late performance.
  • Produces a draft and a list of changes against the base version, so the lawyer reviews only the deviations.

Important: AI assembles the document from verified blocks rather than writing legal text from scratch. Free-form contract generation is a bad idea: the model can invent a clause that sounds right but contradicts the law. How to protect yourself from such inventions is covered in the article on AI hallucinations.

Where it pays off

Wholesale trade, where new supply contracts are signed every week. Agencies — marketing, recruitment, event — with standard service contracts. Rental businesses with dozens of similar contracts. Law and accounting firms, where standard documents are part of the service. The saving on a single document is small, but at thirty contracts a month it adds up to days of working time.

Reviewing counterparty edits

A separate useful feature: the counterparty has sent its own version of the contract, and you need to understand what it changed compared to your template. The model compares the documents and produces a list of changes with a comment on which of them affect money, deadlines and liability. This doesn't replace a lawyer; it saves an hour of careful reading.

Deadline tracking

The third group is the simplest technically and the most underestimated. Deadlines live inside documents: the contract end date, the payment due date, the termination notice period, the date of the next equipment calibration, the license expiry date.

How it works:

  • When any document is uploaded, the model extracts all dates and obligations with their conditions: "give notice 60 days before expiry", "payment within 10 business days of the certificate date".
  • These are used to build the company's calendar of obligations.
  • A set time before each deadline, the person responsible gets a reminder in Telegram with a link to the document and the clause.
  • Once a week the manager receives a summary: what expires in the coming month and what is overdue.

Example: a small coffee shop chain with six lease agreements. Each has its own renewal notice period, from 30 to 90 days. Missing the deadline means either automatic renewal on unfavorable terms or losing the location. A calendar of obligations with reminders removes this risk entirely, and building it doesn't require a complex system — a spreadsheet, an n8n automation and a bot are enough.

How it comes together into one process

The three tasks are rarely launched at the same time. The usual order is this: deadline tracking first — fast, cheap, with an immediate effect. Then recognition of incoming invoices — this is where the accountant saves the most time. And only then contract drafts, because they require a lawyer's work to prepare the templates and the clause library.

The stack for a small business: a mailbox or Telegram as the entry point, n8n as the orchestrator, a multimodal model via API for data extraction, an accounting system or Google Sheets as storage, Telegram for notifications. Where confidentiality is required — a local model such as Qwen on your own server; for document recognition, open models in 2026 already perform acceptably, although they lag behind cloud models on difficult scans.

Where AI in document workflows is not worth using yet

  • Non-standard contracts with individual terms — here AI can help with comparing versions, but not with drafting.
  • Documents where a mistake is irreversible: payment orders for large amounts without a second human check, HR orders.
  • Low volume: with ten invoices a month it is easier to enter them by hand than to set up and maintain recognition.
  • No templates: if every contract in the company is written from scratch, the lawyer creates standard forms first, and AI comes in after that.
