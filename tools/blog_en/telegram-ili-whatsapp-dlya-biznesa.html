The "Telegram or WhatsApp" question comes up at almost every first meeting about a bot. And almost always what's behind it is not a technical choice but uncertainty: "what if we build it where our customers aren't". The concern is justified — a bot in an empty channel won't pay off, no matter how smart it is.

The right answer depends on three things: where your customers already are, what the bot has to do and how much you are willing to pay for each message. Let's go through both messengers against these criteria, without religious preferences, and give you a working framework for choosing.

The main criterion is your market, not the technology

You need to start not with what the platforms can do but with the geography and habits of your audience. Here is the picture we see in 2026 in the markets where we work:

  • Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan — Telegram dominates both personal communication and business. WhatsApp is present, but people hardly expect bots there, and in a number of countries it works unreliably.
  • Georgia, Armenia — a mixed picture. WhatsApp is stronger among older audiences and in everyday services, Telegram among young people and in the IT community. Many businesses have to maintain both.
  • Southern and Western Europe, Latin America, India — WhatsApp has practically no alternative for communicating with customers. People know Telegram but don't use it for business.
  • Germany, the Netherlands, Scandinavia — WhatsApp leads, but a noticeable part of the audience values privacy and is moving to Telegram or Signal. For B2B, it's still more often email.

These are guidelines, not statistics — you need to check them against your own customers. The simplest way: look at where messages to your managers come from now, and ask ten regular customers where they find it more convenient.

How the platforms differ for a bot developer

The difference between the Telegram Bot API and the WhatsApp Business API is fundamental, and it determines not only the cost but also what the bot will be able to do at all.

Access and moderation

A bot in Telegram is created in a minute via @BotFather, for free and without approval. You can launch a test today and rework it tomorrow.

The WhatsApp Business API requires a verified business account with Meta, a connection through an official provider (for example, 360dialog, Twilio or directly via the Cloud API) and compliance with the platform's rules. Outgoing message templates go through moderation — sometimes in hours, sometimes in days. It's not a blocker, but it has to be factored into your timeline.

Who can start the conversation

In Telegram, a bot cannot message a user first — the user has to tap "Start" themselves. After that, the bot can send messages with no limits on time or quantity (within reason).

In WhatsApp, a business can message first, but only with an approved template, and it pays for that. Once the customer replies, a 24-hour "window" opens in which you can communicate freely. When the window closes, it's a template again and payment again. This strongly affects reminder and broadcast scenarios.

Interface and features

Telegram offers more tools for complex scenarios: custom keyboards, inline buttons that update the message, in-chat payments, group bots and Mini Apps — full-fledged web applications inside the messenger. One example of such an app is our Batumi Rent, where the search for rental listings works right in Telegram without installing a separate program. For more on what this format can do, see the article on Telegram Mini Apps for business.

WhatsApp is more restrained: lists, reply buttons (up to three), a product catalog, Flows for forms. That is enough for booking, order confirmation and support, but not for an "app in a chat".

Message costs

The Telegram Bot API is free. You pay only for the server and, if you use one, for the language model.

In WhatsApp, you pay for business-initiated conversations, and the rate depends on the country and the template category (marketing costs more than utility). Roughly, from a few cents to 10–15 cents per conversation, plus the provider's fee. For a base of 5,000 customers, a monthly broadcast can cost hundreds of dollars; in Telegram the same broadcast costs zero.

Scenarios: which messenger wins where

Abstract pros and cons are less useful than specific tasks.

Booking and reminders for a salon, clinic or auto repair shop. If your customers are in Georgia or Europe — WhatsApp: a reminder the day before the visit will arrive where it is sure to be seen. The cost of a utility template is low, and every prevented no-show pays for a month of messages. In the CIS — Telegram, free and with convenient one-button rescheduling.

Online store support. Where customers already write in WhatsApp, the bot should be there too: order status, returns, product questions. For a Telegram audience, it makes sense to add a Mini App with a catalog and in-chat payment.

Loyalty program and broadcasts. Here the economics decide: in WhatsApp every marketing message is paid, and the customer base quickly gets expensive. Telegram lets you communicate with subscribers for free, which is why bonus programs are more often built there. Details are in the article on a bot for a loyalty program.

Internal team processes. Almost always Telegram: free, group chats, rich buttons, no template moderation. WhatsApp is rarely used for internal bots.

B2B and international sales. If your customers are companies in Europe or Latin America, WhatsApp will be perceived as a normal business channel and Telegram as an odd one. In the CIS it's the other way around.

When you need both channels

Businesses in mixed markets — travel agencies, property rentals in Batumi, online schools with students from different countries — often come to the conclusion that there is no need to choose. Technically this is solvable: one bot logic, two messenger "adapters", a shared customer database and a shared queue for operators.

What matters with this approach:

  • Design the scenarios for the more limited platform (WhatsApp) and add extensions in Telegram — otherwise half the features "won't make the move".
  • A single customer record: a person who wrote first in WhatsApp and then in Telegram is one and the same. This is a CRM integration task, covered in the article on integrating a CRM with messengers.
  • Plan broadcasts with cost in mind: in Telegram — to everyone, in WhatsApp — only to the segments where the expected response covers the price.

A two-channel bot costs not twice as much but roughly a third to a half more: the core logic is shared, and only the message delivery layer differs.

Limitations you are rarely warned about

Blocking and availability. In some countries, one of the messengers periodically works unreliably. A bot tied to a single channel stays silent on those days. For critical notifications (payment confirmation, an access code), it's worth having a backup — SMS or email.

Platform rules change. WhatsApp regularly revises its rates and template categories; Telegram introduces limits on mass broadcasts from bots that users mark as spam. The bot needs to be checked for compliance periodically, otherwise sending will suddenly stop.

The phone number as an identifier. In WhatsApp, the customer is a number, which is convenient for matching against the CRM. In Telegram, a user can hide their number, and linking them to a customer record is harder: you need a contact request or a code from their account.

Personal data. For customers from the EU, the conversation history in any messenger is personal data, and the bot must be able to delete it on request. This is the same for both platforms, but it is often forgotten.

A five-minute decision framework

Answer the questions in order — usually the decision becomes clear by the second or third.

  1. Where do customers write from now? If 80% or more come from one messenger, you have your answer: build there.
  2. Do you need to message first? Reminders, status notifications, reactivation — in WhatsApp this is paid and template-based. Calculate the volume and the price.
  3. Do you need an "interface"? A catalog with filters, a customer account, a booking calendar — that's a Telegram Mini App. You can't build this in WhatsApp.
  4. Do customers have a reason to leave their usual messenger? Usually not. Making a European install Telegram for the sake of your bot is a losing strategy.
  5. Are you ready to maintain two channels? If yes and the market is mixed, design for both from the very start; it's cheaper than adding the second one later.

Summary

Telegram wins where free messages, a rich interface and launch speed matter — that is, in the CIS, for internal tasks and for loyalty programs. WhatsApp wins where it is already the channel customers use to talk to businesses — in Europe, Latin America and partly in Georgia. The technology is secondary: a good bot in the wrong channel is worse than a simple bot in the right one.
