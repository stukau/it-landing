n8n, Make or Zapier: Which Automation Platform Should a Small Business Choose?

AI AgentsAugust 19, 2026 · 9 min read

An automation platform is the glue that joins website forms, CRM, messengers, spreadsheets and language models into working processes without writing code for every connection. The three most common options — Zapier, Make and n8n — solve the same problem, but they are built differently, priced differently and behave differently as you grow. The choice between them is rarely obvious, and moving from one to another means rebuilding every workflow.

Below is a comparison on the criteria that really matter to a small business: cost as volume grows, how hard it is to learn, working with data and AI, where the data is stored and who will maintain it all. No rankings and no winner — instead, three company profiles and the right choice for each.

Each platform in brief

Zapier — the oldest and the simplest. A huge number of ready-made integrations, a "trigger — action" principle, a minimal learning curve. A cloud service, billed by the number of tasks executed. Good for linear workflows: a lead comes in — create a card — send an email.

Make (formerly Integromat) — a visual builder with branching, loops and array processing. A workflow is drawn on a canvas like a diagram. Cloud-based, billed by operations. It lets you build noticeably more complex logic than Zapier with comparable simplicity.

n8n — an open-source builder. You can use the cloud version or deploy it on your own server — in which case you don't pay for operations. The most flexible: you can write code in any node, there are built-in nodes for working with language models and agents, and there are no limits on the volume of data processed other than the server's capacity. The learning curve is steeper.

Criterion 1. What it costs as you grow

The main difference is in the pricing model.

  • Zapier counts tasks: every step of a workflow after the trigger. A five-step workflow that ran a thousand times is five thousand tasks. At small volumes it's inexpensive; at hundreds of leads a day the bill becomes noticeable.
  • Make counts operations in a similar way, but the plans are on average more lenient, and processing an array of data within a single run is billed more favorably.
  • n8n in the cloud version counts whole workflow runs, regardless of the number of steps inside. On your own server it's only the cost of the server, roughly from a few dollars a month for a virtual machine.

A practical guide: while the automation runs hundreds of times a month, the difference in money is small and you should choose by convenience. At tens of thousands of operations a month, self-hosted n8n becomes noticeably cheaper, and the gap grows with volume. But to the cost of the server you need to add the time spent administering it — your own or a contractor's.

Criterion 2. Who will be using it

If the workflows will be set up and edited by the owner or an office manager with no technical experience, Zapier can be learned in an evening, Make in a few days, n8n in one to two weeks given the motivation. The difference isn't that n8n is "harder," but that it shows more: data structure, request headers, expressions. To someone without a technical background, this is noise at first.

If the automation is built by a contractor and the company only uses the result, the criterion changes: what matters more is how easily another specialist can later make sense of the workflows. Here all three platforms are roughly equal, provided the workflows are clearly named and documented. More on the limits of building it yourself in our article on no-code automation.

Criterion 3. Complexity of the logic

A linear workflow with no conditions — Zapier will cope. Conditions, branching, loops over a list of order items, merging data from several sources — Make does this clearly. Workflows where you need to process large arrays, call external APIs with non-standard authorization, add small snippets of code to transform data, or keep state between runs — n8n.

An example of where the line falls: an online store wants, on every order, to check stock for all items in the accounting system, recalculate delivery times by region and send the customer a message with the result. In Zapier this turns into a cumbersome chain with limitations. In Make it's doable, but with twenty items in an order the operations will be used up quickly. In n8n it's one run with a loop inside.

Criterion 4. Working with AI and agents

All three platforms can call GPT-5, Claude, Gemini and other models. The difference is in depth.

  • In Zapier, a model call is a single step: pass in text, get an answer. For classifying leads or drafting a reply from a template, that's enough.
  • In Make — the same plus convenient handling of the result: parsing a structured response, branching on the result.
  • In n8n there are dedicated nodes for agents: a model with tools, conversation memory, connections to vector databases (Qdrant, Pinecone) for RAG, call chains. You can build a Telegram bot that answers from company documents and calls the CRM with no external code.

If what you're planning is an agent — with conversation, memory and tools — and not one-off model calls inside a linear process, n8n is currently noticeably more convenient. This is one of the reasons we at AiKrok use it most often for AI projects. How such agents work in principle is covered in our article on business automation with AI agents.

Criterion 5. Where the data is stored

Zapier and Make are cloud services, and all workflow data passes through their servers: leads, correspondence, payment and company details. For most companies this is acceptable at the same level as a cloud CRM. But if there are requirements to keep data inside the country or inside the company — healthcare, finance, legal services, government contracts — the only option of the three is n8n on your own server in the required jurisdiction. Then the platform, the logs and the intermediate data all stay under your control.

Criterion 6. Reliability and debugging

With cloud platforms, reliability is the provider's concern; outages are rare, but you can't influence them. With n8n on your own server, reliability is your concern: you need backups, monitoring and updates. On the other hand, the execution logs are complete and stay with you for as long as you need; on cloud plans the history is often limited in time.

Debugging is visual on all three platforms: you can see at which step the workflow failed and with what data. In n8n you can re-run an execution with the same input data, which speeds up finding errors.

Three profiles and the choice for each

A yoga studio, 3 employees

Tasks: a website lead goes into a spreadsheet and to the administrator in Telegram, a reminder to the customer the day before a class, a news mailing. Volume — dozens of events a day. No technical specialist. The choice is Zapier or Make: fast, cheap at this volume, and the administrator can manage it herself. A move to something else will only be needed if the studio grows into a chain.

An online store, 12 employees, 100–300 orders a day

Tasks: orders from several channels into the accounting system, stock checks, status updates to customers, returns, a daily summary. Logic with branching, volume — thousands of operations a day. The choice is Make, if the team wants to edit workflows itself and is prepared to pay for operations, or n8n on your own server, if there's a contractor or a technically savvy employee and cost at scale matters. At these volumes the difference in the monthly fee is already substantial. Store automation is covered separately in our article on automating an online store.

A clinic, 40 employees, an AI assistant for patients

Tasks: an agent in Telegram and WhatsApp answers questions, books appointments and sends reminders; patient data must not leave the clinic's perimeter. The choice is n8n on your own server, with a local or cloud model depending on which data passes through each workflow. The other options are ruled out by the data requirements.

Can you combine them

Yes, and it's normal practice. Simple workflows that the office manager edits go in Make; agents and sensitive data go in n8n. But two tools mean double the maintenance and two points of failure, so it's worth doing deliberately, not out of inertia.

Where to start

  1. Calculate the volume: how many events a day will trigger the automation in six months' time, not today.
  2. Determine who will edit the workflows: a non-technical employee, a technically savvy employee or a contractor.
  3. Check the data requirements: are there restrictions on processing in the cloud.
  4. Decide whether you need an agent with a conversation or whether a model call inside a linear process is enough.
  5. Build the first workflow on the chosen platform within a week and look at operation usage and convenience — it will tell you more than any comparison.

If the first point gives large volumes, the third gives restrictions and the fourth gives an agent, the choice is almost certainly n8n. If it's "no" everywhere, start with Make or Zapier and don't overcomplicate things.
