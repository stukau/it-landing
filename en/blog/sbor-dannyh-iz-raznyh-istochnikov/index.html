How to Bring Data from Ads, CRM and Inventory into One Picture

AnalyticsSeptember 20, 2026 · 8 min read

The marketer shows a report from the ad account: 340 leads, and the cost per lead has gone down. The head of sales opens the CRM: 210 leads, half of them unqualified. The accountant sends an export from 1C: there are fewer payments than closed deals in the CRM. Three reports, three truths, and none of them answers the owner's question, "how much did we earn from this advertising". This article is about how to connect scattered systems into one picture — without a corporate data warehouse or a team of analysts.

Why the numbers don't match

Before connecting any tools, it's worth understanding the nature of the discrepancies, otherwise the combined data will be just as contradictory, only in one window.

Different definitions. For the ad account, a "lead" is a submitted form. For the CRM it's a created record, and duplicates with the same phone number may or may not be counted. For 1C a lead doesn't exist at all; there is an invoice and a payment.

Different points in time. The click was on the 28th, the lead on the 29th, the deal closed on the 15th of the following month, the payment arrived on the 20th. Which month should the revenue from this advertising be recorded in? Each system answers in its own way.

Different keys. Advertising knows the click ID, the CRM knows the phone number and name, the warehouse knows the order number. If there is no shared field between them, they cannot be linked, no matter which tool you buy.

The practical conclusion: combining data is 30% a technical task and 70% an agreement on what we count and how.

The three layers of any data collection setup

Regardless of scale, a working setup consists of three levels.

Sources

The systems where data originates: ad accounts, the CRM (amoCRM, Bitrix24, HubSpot), the accounting system (1C or an equivalent), the payment service, the website with analytics, the warehouse software, and sometimes Google Sheets where managers keep something of their own. Nothing in the sources needs to be changed; the task is to pull data from them regularly.

Storage

One place where everything is put in a single format. For a small business this is usually PostgreSQL on an inexpensive server or BigQuery in the cloud. At the very start Google Sheets may be enough, but with caveats, covered below.

Presentation layer

The layer people see: a dashboard in Metabase, Looker Studio or Power BI, a daily summary in Telegram, an AI assistant's answers to questions. The presentation layer reads from the storage, not from the sources directly — this is the key difference from the "let's connect the dashboard straight to the CRM" approach.

Which sources are connected first

The experience of small companies is fairly uniform. The first wave is almost always the same.

  • Advertising. Spend, impressions, clicks by campaign and day. Pulled through the ad accounts' APIs, in most cases automatically once a day.
  • CRM. Deals, stages, amounts, sources, owners, stage change dates. It's important to pull the history of transitions between stages, not just the current state — otherwise you can't calculate funnel velocity.
  • Accounting system. Shipments, payments, returns, stock levels. There is a separate article on the specifics of exporting from 1C: integration with 1C and accounting systems.
  • Website. Visits, conversions, UTM tags. Needed mainly to link advertising to leads.

The second wave is what gets added a couple of months later: reviews, support inquiries, delivery data, purchase cost for calculating margin.

The main question: how to link records between systems

This is where most projects either succeed or don't. You need a chain of identifiers that leads from the ad click to the payment.

A working setup for a company that sells through a website and managers looks like this:

  1. The ad passes UTM tags and a click ID into the link.
  2. The form on the website writes these tags into hidden fields and sends them to the CRM together with the phone number.
  3. The CRM stores the phone number in normalized form (one format for all) and the deal ID.
  4. When an invoice is issued in 1C, the deal number from the CRM is written into the comment or a separate field.
  5. The payment in 1C is tied to the invoice, and therefore through the invoice to the deal, through the deal to the tag, and through the tag to the campaign.

If even one link is missing, the chain breaks. It most often breaks at the fourth step: managers issue invoices without a reference to the CRM. This is solved not technically but with a procedure and a check: a deal without the link is not considered closed.

For an offline business (a salon, a clinic, a store), the linking key is usually the customer's phone number, and then a single format for recording it in all systems is critical.

How to pull the data: tools

Three options are realistic for a small business, and they can be combined.

Automation platforms. n8n, Make or Zapier can call the APIs of ad accounts and the CRM on a schedule, transform the data and write it to the database. This is the fastest route: a typical scenario, "once a day pull spend by campaign and put it in a table", is built in a few hours.

Built-in BI connectors. Looker Studio and Power BI connect to a number of sources directly. Convenient for a first look, but the data is not saved in your own storage, and as soon as you need to join two sources by the customer's phone number, the possibilities run out.

Custom scripts. Small Python programs that do the export, cleaning and loading. They are needed where a system has no ready-made connector or where the transformation logic is complex: for example, parsing 1C exports with a non-standard structure.

At AiKrok we usually start with n8n on the client's server: it's free, the data doesn't go to a third party, and when complex logic appears, scripts are added alongside it.

Google Sheets or a real database

A question that comes up almost every time. Spreadsheets are appealing for their simplicity: everyone knows how to use them, and no server is needed.

Google Sheets is suitable if there is little data (roughly up to a few tens of thousands of rows), there are two or three sources and nobody edits the data by hand. As soon as someone "tweaks" a row, the automatic report no longer matches the source, and trust in it disappears.

PostgreSQL or BigQuery is the right choice if history accumulates over years, you need joins between tables by keys, and you plan AI analytics on top — a model writes SQL queries against a database far more reliably than formulas for a spreadsheet. How to then ask questions of the collected data is described in detail in the article about AI analytics.

A compromise that often works: a database as the storage and spreadsheets as the presentation layer for those who are more used to them.

Where this won't work, or won't work right away

  • If the deal source isn't recorded in the CRM, no tool will show the return on advertising. Discipline first, then integration.
  • If the business sells by phone without a website, a call can only be linked to advertising through call tracking — that's a separate system and a separate cost.
  • If the books are kept in a boxed version of 1C on the accountant's computer, an export is possible but will require a 1C specialist and will be less reliable than from the cloud version.
  • If the data is needed not daily but once a quarter, automation may not pay off — sometimes it's cheaper to reconcile it by hand once every three months.

A plan for the first month

A realistic sequence for a company with a CRM, advertising and an accounting system:

  1. Week 1. A list of metrics with definitions. An inventory of sources: what, from where, whether there is an API. A decision on linking keys.
  2. Week 2. The storage is deployed, advertising and the CRM are connected, data arrives on a schedule. The first simple dashboard: spend and leads by day.
  3. Week 3. The accounting system is connected, and the link between deals and payments is set up. The number it was all started for appears: revenue by advertising source.
  4. Week 4. Checking discrepancies, refining the procedure for managers, a morning summary in Telegram for the owner. Which metrics to include in it is described in our article about the owner's dashboard.

The main result of the first month is not a pretty dashboard but one number that the marketer, sales and the accountant all believe. Everything else is built on top of it.
