llms.txt, schema.org and robots.txt: How to Get Your Website Ready for AI Search

WebsitesSeptember 23, 2026 · 8 min read

A website can be attractive, fast and rank well in ordinary search, yet to an AI assistant remain a collection of images and scripts from which it's impossible to work out what the company does and how much its services cost. Three small files and one layer of markup solve most of this problem: robots.txt determines who is let in, schema.org explains what exactly is written on the page, and llms.txt gives the assistant a short map of the site in a language it understands. This article is a practical guide for a business owner who wants to brief a developer and understand what they will get.

Three tools, three different jobs

They are often confused, so let's start by telling them apart.

  • robots.txt — a file in the site root that tells bots which sections they may read. It has existed for more than twenty years, but in recent years new "guests" have appeared in it: the bots of AI companies.
  • schema.org — a markup standard that labels the content of a page: this is the organization's name, this is the address, this is the price, this is a question and an answer. An ordinary visitor doesn't see the markup; for a bot it provides structure in place of a wall of text.
  • llms.txt — a relatively new convention: a text file in which the site briefly describes itself and lists its key pages for language models. The standard is not yet universal, but it costs little and does no harm.

None of them guarantees a place in assistants' answers. They remove the technical obstacles, and from there the content does the work. How assistants choose who to name is covered in our article on AI Visibility.

robots.txt: who to let in and who not to

Most AI companies have their own bots with recognizable names, for example OpenAI's GPTBot, Anthropic's ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, and Google-Extended for training Google's models. Some of them read a site to train models, others to answer with real-time search. These are different things, and you can treat them differently.

Typical situations:

  • The site is open to everyone. The default for most commercial sites: you want assistants to know about you. What needs checking is the opposite — whether the developer or the hosting has blocked these bots by accident. It happens more often than you'd think, especially on site-builder websites with "bot protection" switched on.
  • Open for search, closed for training. A compromise for those who don't want their content used in model training but do want to appear in answers. Technically these are different bots, and rules can be set for them separately. A caveat: not all companies observe this separation equally strictly.
  • Everything closed. A deliberate choice for sites with unique paid content. For a local business that needs customers, this choice is rarely justified.

Practical advice: ask your developer to show you the current robots.txt and check whether it contains a blanket ban that also blocks assistant bots. It takes ten minutes, and the effect is sometimes greater than a month of work on content.

schema.org: telling the page what's on it

The markup is added to the page code in JSON-LD format — a small block describing entities. For a business website, a few types matter.

Organization or LocalBusiness

Name, address, phone, opening hours, service area, links to social media and map profiles. For a local business there are more specific types that fit: Dentist, Restaurant, RealEstateAgent and the like. This markup helps an assistant link mentions of you on different platforms into a single entity.

Service and Product

What you sell, with a description and, where possible, a price or a range. Price markup is especially important: assistants readily answer "how much does it cost" questions, and a structured price inspires more trust than a figure somewhere in the text.

FAQPage

Question-and-answer pairs. If the site has real customer questions with detailed answers, the markup lets an assistant take the answer in full. The condition: the questions must be real and the answers substantive. Marking up three advertising phrases dressed as an FAQ is useless.

Article and BlogPosting

For blog articles: headline, date, author, description. The date is especially important: assistants with search take freshness into account, and an article without a date loses to an article with one.

The markup must match what is visible on the page. If the markup shows one price and the text another, trust in both drops.

llms.txt: a site map for language models

The idea is simple: in the site root sits a file called llms.txt which says, in plain text in Markdown format, what the site is and lists the main pages with links and short annotations. A language model that lands on the site can read it first and immediately understand where to look for an answer, without having to parse menus and scripts.

For a small company, the file structure looks roughly like this:

  1. A heading with the company name.
  2. One or two paragraphs: who you are, where you operate, what you do and for whom, what you don't do.
  3. A "Services" section with links to the service pages and a one-line description of each.
  4. A "Prices and terms" section, if there are separate pages.
  5. A "Frequently asked questions" or "Useful articles" section with the key materials.
  6. Contacts.

An extended version, llms-full.txt, is often placed alongside it, with the content of the key pages given in full as text. This is useful for sites whose main text is loaded by scripts and is poorly read by bots.

An honest caveat: support for llms.txt among assistants is uneven, and there is no guarantee that a particular bot will read it. But writing such a file forces you to state what you do in one place and without filler — which is useful in itself, and the cost of implementation is close to zero.

What else stops assistants from reading a site

Files and markup won't help if the site itself is built in a way that makes its text impossible to extract.

  • The text is rendered by scripts. Sites on some frameworks serve an empty page and fill in the content in the browser. Many bots don't execute scripts and see nothing. The solution is server-side rendering or static page generation.
  • Prices and terms only in PDFs and images. An assistant won't read a price list in image form. You need a text version on the page.
  • One long landing page for everything. It's easier for an assistant to cite a page devoted to one question than to hunt for the right paragraph among twenty blocks.
  • No "About" page with facts. City, year founded, specialization, team. This is the first thing an assistant looks for to understand who you are.
  • Headings with no meaning. "Why us" and "Our advantages" carry no information. "Timeline and cost of Telegram bot development" does.

How to check the result

After implementation, it's worth making sure everything works and isn't just sitting on the server.

  • Open robots.txt and llms.txt in a browser at their direct addresses and make sure they are accessible.
  • Check the markup with a structured data validator: it will show errors and warnings.
  • Look at the page "through a bot's eyes": disable script execution in the browser and check whether the text is visible.
  • After a few weeks, ask assistants questions about the company and compare with the result before the changes. If the site has its own AI assistant, the same order in the content will improve its answers too — it reads the same pages.

A checklist for briefing your developer

To put briefly what needs to be done:

  1. robots.txt has been checked, AI assistant bots are not blocked by accident; a deliberate decision has been made on training bots.
  2. Organization or local business markup has been added with full contact details.
  3. Services and products are marked up, with prices where possible.
  4. Real questions and answers are marked up, as are blog articles with dates.
  5. An llms.txt has been created with a description of the company and links to the key pages.
  6. Page text is available without executing scripts; prices and terms have been moved from PDF into HTML.
  7. A month later, the assistants' answers have been checked again.
