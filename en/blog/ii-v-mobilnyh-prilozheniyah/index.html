AI in Mobile Apps: Receipt Recognition, Voice Input and On-Device Data

AppsSeptember 27, 2026 · 8 min read

"Add AI to the app" is a request heard at every second mobile development meeting in 2026. But it rarely comes with an understanding of what exactly the AI should do and how that will help the person holding the phone. Meanwhile, there are three scenarios where AI in a mobile app delivers tangible value: recognizing receipts and documents with the camera, voice input instead of forms, and smart data processing right on the device. Let's go through each with examples, discuss what to compute on the phone and what in the cloud, and look at what it costs to run.

What AI really changes in a mobile app

The main value of AI in an app is not chat. Users rarely want to talk to an expense tracking app. They want data entry to take seconds, not minutes. The value of AI is that it turns unstructured input — a photo, speech, a screenshot — into structured data the app knows how to work with.

Compare two ways of adding a purchase to a budget. The first: open the app, choose a category, enter the amount, choose the store, enter the date, save — five screens. The second: photograph the receipt, see the parsed list with amounts, tap "save." The second way makes the app something people use every day rather than abandon after a week.

Scenario 1: recognizing receipts and documents

A classic example is My Stash, a family budget app that we at AiKrok released for iOS. The user photographs a store receipt and a few seconds later sees a list of items with prices, the total, the store name and the date, with the items already sorted into categories.

How it works inside:

  1. The camera takes a picture, and the app crops and straightens the image and increases the contrast. This step runs on the device.
  2. The image is passed to a vision-capable model (Claude, GPT-5, Gemini or the open Qwen-VL) with an instruction to return a structured result: list of items, amounts, total, store, date.
  3. The model's response is validated: the sum of the items matches the total, the dates are within an acceptable range, numbers are numbers. If validation fails, the user is shown the result with a "please check" note.
  4. The user confirms or corrects, and the data is saved.

To be honest about accuracy: on flat receipts with clear print, modern models parse the items almost without errors. On crumpled, faded or angled ones there are errors, most often in individual digits. That is why the confirmation step is mandatory, and the interface must let the user fix one line without retaking the whole photo.

The same approach works for delivery notes, business cards, contracts and labels. For a business this could be, for example, an app for a sales representative who photographs the stock on the shelf, or an app for a delivery driver who photographs delivery notes on handover.

Scenario 2: voice input that understands meaning

Voice input in apps has existed for a long time, but it used to simply turn speech into text, after which the user still filled in the fields. The AI version goes further: speech becomes a structured action.

In SubMap, a subscription tracker, the user says: "Add a music subscription, nine ninety-nine a month, charged on the fifth." The app creates a record with the name, amount, billing period and date — without a single tap on the form fields. In My Stash, shopping lists are created the same way: "milk, bread, two kilos of apples and something to go with tea" becomes a list of four items.

The chain here has two stages: first speech recognition (on the device using the system or through a cloud service), then a language model extracts structure from the text according to a defined schema. The second stage is what distinguishes "smart" input from dictation.

Where voice works worse: noisy places, professional terminology and brand names, short commands without context. A good practice is to show the recognized result before saving and let the user correct it by voice as well: "not the fifth, the fifteenth."

Scenario 3: data on the device

Finance, health, personal notes — these are categories where users increasingly ask where their data goes. For apps with AI the question is especially sharp: if every receipt is sent to a cloud model, then someone outside the phone sees what you buy.

There are three approaches, and they can be combined:

  • Everything on the device. Data is stored locally and processed by the platform's built-in models (on iOS this is Core ML and the system frameworks, on Android their equivalents). This suits speech recognition, classification and simple entity extraction. Full parsing of a receipt by a complex model on the phone is still limited by the device's capabilities, although the boundary is shifting quickly.
  • Processing in the cloud, storage on the device. The image is sent to the model, the result is returned and saved locally, and nothing remains on the server. This is how My Stash works: the purchase history lives only on the user's phone. It is a compromise that suits most people.
  • Your own model on your own server. For corporate apps with sensitive data: an open model (Qwen, Llama, Mistral) is deployed on the company's server, and the data doesn't leave its perimeter. It is more expensive to maintain, but it is the only option for a number of industries. More on the choice in the article on data security when implementing AI.

Whatever you choose, you need to tell the user directly: where the photos go, what is stored and where. Lack of transparency on this point puts people off more than an honest "the receipt is processed by a cloud model and is not saved on the server."

Choosing a model: cloud or device

The decision depends on three factors: the complexity of the task, the sensitivity of the data and the running budget.

Cloud models (Claude, GPT-5, Gemini) are stronger at complex tasks: parsing a receipt with dozens of items, understanding free speech with recognition errors, extracting structure from a badly photographed document. The price is a fee for every request and dependence on the network.

On-device models cost nothing to run, work without the internet and don't send data outside. But they handle a narrower range of tasks and require a modern phone. For speech recognition, determining a purchase category from its name and simple classification, they are usually enough.

A practical setup: simple tasks on the device, complex ones in the cloud, with a switch the user can understand or at least an explanation in the settings.

What it costs to run

Development is a separate topic; this is about costs after launch. Cloud models are billed by the volume of text and images processed. As a guide: parsing one receipt or one voice command costs a fraction of a cent, which means a user who adds ten receipts a day costs roughly a few dozen cents a month. For an app with a thousand active users, that is tens, rarely hundreds, of dollars a month — and this amount grows predictably with the audience.

What increases costs unexpectedly: sending images at full resolution (they should be compressed), repeat requests after errors, long model instructions in every request. All of this is solved at the development stage if you think about it.

For monetized apps, AI costs are built into the subscription: a free monthly limit of recognitions and unlimited use in the paid version is already a familiar practice.

Interface rules for AI features

A few principles we have worked out on our own apps:

  • Always show the result before saving. AI makes mistakes; the user must be able to see that and fix it in one tap.
  • Correcting is cheaper than repeating. Fix one digit in the amount rather than retake the receipt photo.
  • Clear waiting. Processing takes seconds — the interface should show what is happening and not block the rest of the app.
  • Working without AI. If there is no network or the limit has been used up, manual entry must remain available. AI speeds things up; it doesn't replace them.
  • Learning from corrections. If the user moves "coffee" from the "groceries" category to "cafes" three times, the app should remember that, at least locally.

Where an app doesn't need AI

If the data is already structured (the order comes from your own system), if the user enters one number once a day, if the audience is internal employees with an established process — AI will add cost and complexity without noticeable benefit. A good test: how many taps does the AI feature save in the most frequent scenario? If fewer than three, it is probably not needed.

Where to start

  1. Find the most frequent data entry in the app that currently takes more than a minute.
  2. Determine which unstructured source will replace it: a photo, voice, a screenshot.
  3. Decide what is sensitive in this data and where it should be processed.
  4. Build one AI feature with mandatory confirmation of the result and measure whether entry has become faster and whether people use it more often.
