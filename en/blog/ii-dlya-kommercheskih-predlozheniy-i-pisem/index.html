AI for Sales Proposals and Emails: Personalization Without a Copywriter

AI AgentsAugust 24, 2026 · 8 min read

In a small company's sales team, a proposal is almost always produced in one of two ways. Either the manager takes last year's file, changes the client's name and forgets to fix a couple of paragraphs that have nothing to do with this client. Or they sit down and write from scratch, spend an hour and a half, and the proposal goes out the next day, when the client has already received three others. Follow-up emails after meetings are the same story: "I'll write it tonight" turns into "I'll write it tomorrow" and then into silence.

In 2026, language models write business text as well as the average manager and do it in seconds. But there is a big difference between "ChatGPT wrote us a proposal" and a working personalization system. Let's look at how to build a process in which AI prepares proposals and emails for each client, and the manager only reviews and sends.

Why "just ask the model to write it" doesn't work

The first experience is the same for many: a manager opens a chat with the model, types "write a proposal for the supply of office furniture" and gets smooth text that could be sent to anyone — which is exactly why nobody needs it. Clients read texts like this every day and recognize them from the first paragraph.

Personalization is not about style, it is about data. For a proposal to land with a client, the model has to know: what the company is, what it does, what request it sent, what was discussed at the meeting, what its budget and timing constraints are, what we have sold to similar clients. None of this is available to the model in a chat. All of it is in the CRM, in the correspondence, in the manager's notes. The system's job is to collect this data and give it to the model together with the company's template and rules.

So the right way to frame the task is not "AI writes the proposal" but "AI assembles a draft proposal from the deal data, the price list and our wording library."

What exactly AI does well

  • A draft proposal in the company's structure. An opening paragraph about the client's task in the client's own words, the scope of the solution, a calculation based on the price list, timelines, terms, the next step. The model doesn't invent a structure; it fills in yours.
  • A follow-up email after a meeting. Based on the call transcript or the manager's notes: what was discussed, what was agreed, what we are doing, what we expect from the client. An email like this takes the model seconds, and takes the manager half an hour that they don't have.
  • A series of reminders. The client hasn't replied to the proposal for three days — a gentle email to check in. Another five days — an email with something extra of value, such as a case study from their industry. The texts are different, not a copy of "a reminder about our proposal."
  • Adapting to the reader's role. The same proposal for the owner is about money and timelines; for the CTO it is about integrations and reliability. The model shifts the emphasis and leaves the facts alone.
  • Translation and localization. For a business that works in Georgia, Kazakhstan or with European clients, a proposal in two or three languages without a translator is a noticeable saving, provided the manager knows the language well enough to check it.
  • Answers to standard questions in correspondence. Payment, delivery and warranty terms — the model words the answer from the company's knowledge base in the manager's tone.

What AI should not do

The limits matter more than the capabilities here, because a mistake in a proposal is not a typo but a promise the company is accountable for.

Prices, discounts, timelines. The model doesn't calculate them itself and doesn't take them "approximately." The system does the calculation from the price list and the rules; the model only inserts the finished numbers into the text. If an item is missing from the price list, the draft gets a note for the manager, not a plausible-looking figure. Why this is critical is explained in the article on AI hallucinations.

Legal terms. Wording on liability, warranties and returns comes from an approved library of blocks. The model picks the right block but doesn't rewrite it.

Sending without review. A draft always goes through the manager. That takes two to three minutes instead of an hour and a half of writing, and those minutes are enough to catch a factual error.

Factual claims about the client. The model may suggest "you are expanding your network in the regions" based on a phrase from the correspondence. If that is a guess, it must be marked as an assumption or removed.

How the process works: from deal to sent email

Here is a typical setup that we at AiKrok build for sales teams of three to ten people.

  1. Trigger. The manager moves the deal in amoCRM, Bitrix24 or HubSpot to the "prepare proposal" stage or clicks a button in the deal card. For follow-up emails after meetings, the trigger is a completed call with a transcript or a manager's note.
  2. Collecting context. The automation (n8n or Make) pulls company data, correspondence history, notes, the selected items and quantities from the CRM. Prices come from the price list. Suitable blocks come from the library: case studies from this industry, terms, service descriptions.
  3. Generation. The model receives the proposal template, the collected context, the tone rules and the list of prohibitions, and returns a draft. For proposals it is convenient to get it in structured form right away, so it can then be laid out as a PDF or a document in the company's branded template.
  4. Review. The draft appears in the deal card or is sent to the manager in Telegram. They edit and confirm it.
  5. Sending and logging. The email goes to the client, a copy and the date are saved in the CRM, and a reminder task is set for a given number of days later.

This setup is part of broader sales automation: proposals and emails are built into the funnel instead of living separately in the manager's folder.

The wording library — where quality begins

This is the most underestimated part of the project. The model writes exactly as well as the examples it relies on. So before launch it is worth collecting:

  • five to ten of the company's best proposals from recent years — the ones after which deals were closed;
  • short case studies by industry in a single format: the task, what was done, the result without made-up numbers;
  • approved legal and commercial blocks;
  • a description of the tone: how the company addresses clients, which words it avoids, how long its emails are;
  • examples of bad proposals with notes on exactly what is wrong with them.

This takes a day or two of the head of sales' time, and it is the most valuable investment in the project. Without a library the model will write about "unique comprehensive solutions"; with it, it writes in your language.

Example: an equipment supplier for cafes

The company sells coffee machines, refrigeration equipment and furniture for food service businesses, with five managers and 60–80 requests a month. Proposals took two to three hours each, and some requests never got a proposal at all.

They put a setup in place: the manager marks the items and quantities in the CRM, the automation pulls in the prices, the model writes the introduction based on the client's request and picks a case study from the library — one for a coffee shop, another for a canteen. The draft arrives in the deal card, and the manager edits and sends it. After a week without a reply, a short email with a question goes out; after two weeks, an email offering a calculation for a different budget.

What changed in practice: proposal preparation time dropped to 15–20 minutes including review, no request is left without a proposal, and reminders go out without the managers' involvement. What didn't change: the model did not raise the proposal-to-deal conversion rate by itself — that depends on prices and terms, not on the text. The gains were in speed and coverage.

What to expect and what not to expect

Realistic expectations for the project:

  • Time spent on proposals and emails drops several times over — this is the most reliable effect.
  • Text quality evens out: a newcomer writes at the level of an experienced manager because they rely on the same library.
  • No email gets lost: reminders go out on schedule.
  • Conversion grows only if proposals used to go out late or not at all. If the problem is the price or the product, text won't solve it.
  • For the first two to three weeks managers will edit a lot — this is a normal period of tuning the prompts and the library.

Where to start

You don't need to build everything into the CRM right away. A workable first step is to assemble the wording library and launch generation of follow-up emails after meetings: this is the most frequent and the simplest document, a mistake in it is cheap, and the time saving is visible immediately. Once managers get used to reviewing drafts, you add proposals with price list calculations and a series of reminders.

If the company has no CRM or the data in it is not maintained, that is where to start — see the article on choosing a CRM for a small business. Personalization without client data is just nice words.
